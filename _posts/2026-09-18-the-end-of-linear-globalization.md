  <!-- ========================================================================= -->
  <section id="chap-intro" class="monograph-chapter active">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section I</span>
      <h2>The End of Linear Globalization: The Fractured Consensus</h2>
    </div>

    <!-- Primer Box for High Schoolers & General Readers -->
    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
        <span style="font-size: 0.8rem; color: var(--text-muted);">Reading Lens</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">Why didn't world trade bring permanent global peace?</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        In the 1990s, after the Soviet Union collapsed, political thinkers believed the entire world would soon look the same: democratic, capitalist, and happily trading across open borders. Factories moved overseas, shipping containers crisscrossed oceans, and money flowed without limits. But that dream ignored human nature and power politics. Today, countries are using their trade pipelines, microchips, and natural resources as weapons. Globalization isn't disappearing, but it has stopped moving in a smooth, straight line—it is fragmenting into defensive regional blocs.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concept:</strong> <span class="term-chip" data-term="end_of_history">The End of History</span> was the belief that free markets and democracy had permanently defeated all rival political models.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      When the Cold War came to an end, Francis Fukuyama (<a href="#ref-fukuyama" class="citation-link">1992</a>) famously described it as the <span class="term-chip" data-term="end_of_history">"end of history."</span> His idea was that liberal democracy and market-based globalization would dominate the world, pushing out ideological conflict for good. That belief helped drive a wave of optimism in the 1990s and early 2000s, years marked by the fast growth of multilateral institutions, the free movement of capital, and deeply integrated supply chains (<a href="#ref-frieden" class="citation-link">Frieden, 2006</a>; <a href="#ref-sachs" class="citation-link">Sachs, 2005</a>).
    </p>

    <!-- Editorial Figure 1: Cargo Ship at Sea -->
    <figure class="monograph-figure">
      <img src="{{ '/assets/images/cargo-ship.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="Massive container cargo ship navigating open ocean waters at dusk">
      <figcaption class="monograph-caption">
        <span><strong>Figure 1.1:</strong> Global Maritime Chokepoints &amp; Containerized Supply Chains.</span>
        <span>Source: International Maritime Logistics Archive</span>
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>
      But the 21st century hasn't followed that idea. Instead, it's been defined by the return of nationalism, rising strategic tensions, and regional conflicts that have challenged the post-Cold War order (<a href="#ref-crawford" class="citation-link">Crawford, 2023</a>). The unipolar moment that followed 1991, once led by U.S. power and neoliberal economic thinking, has fractured. Now, the global stage is shaped by renewed great-power rivalry and economic nationalism that threaten to unravel the very systems that once upheld globalization (<a href="#ref-owen" class="citation-link">Owen, Brzezinski, &amp; Okita, 1984</a>).
    </p>

    <div class="case-card-quote" style="margin: 1.75rem 0; border-left-color: var(--accent-color); font-size: 1.05rem;">
      <strong>"States are once again relying on physical geography, chokepoints, energy pipelines, and strategic natural resources to assert control."</strong>
    </div>

    <p>
      Today's economic battles also play out in cyberspace, and through regulation; tariffs on semiconductors, data localization laws, and even digital espionage are now part of the mix. Leaders now face tough choices between efficiency and resilience, growth and sustainability, openness and control. Globalization isn't vanishing, but it is transforming into a posture of <span class="term-chip" data-term="defensive_globalization">defensive globalization</span>.
    </p>

  </section>

  <section id="chap-theory" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section II</span>
      <h2>Theory &amp; Geopolitics: Power Dynamics and Vulnerabilities</h2>
    </div>

    <!-- Primer Box -->
    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">How thinkers explain the clash between money and territory</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        Economists like to imagine that trade flows freely like water, finding the cheapest and most efficient route anywhere on Earth. But geopolitical theorists know that trade moves through real territory—narrow ocean passages, pipelines, and fiber-optic cables controlled by sovereign governments. When tension flares, governments quickly realize that the same trade connections that made them rich also make them vulnerable to blockades, digital espionage, and sanctions.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concept:</strong> <span class="term-chip" data-term="trilemma">Rodrik's Globalization Trilemma</span> states that you cannot simultaneously have global integration, complete national sovereignty, and democratic choice. You must pick two.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      The interaction between geopolitical competition and economic theory has profoundly influenced the structure and vulnerabilities of global trade and investment. Insights from both disciplines help explain how power dynamics, territorial imperatives, and technological shifts shape international economic relations (<a href="#ref-krugman-1990" class="citation-link">Krugman, 1990</a>; <a href="#ref-harris" class="citation-link">Harris Foundation, 1937</a>).
    </p>

    <!-- Four Pillars Theorist Cards -->
    <div class="case-card" style="margin-top: 1.5rem;">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">John A. Agnew &bull; Political Landscapes &amp; Relational Geography</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">Relational Geopolitics</span>
      </div>
      <p>
        Agnew (<a href="#ref-agnew" class="citation-link">2007</a>) challenges the static, state-centric "territorial trap," arguing that geopolitical power emerges through dynamic interactions and networked spatial ties. Concepts like <span class="term-chip" data-term="political_landscapes">political landscapes</span> explain how emerging powers (such as India and Brazil) reconfigure global trade through geographic and historical claims, creating multi-polar trading corridors that defy traditional Western-dominated institutions.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="case-card">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">Peter Kelly &bull; Classical Geopolitics &amp; Maritime Imperatives</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">Territorial Control</span>
      </div>
      <p>
        Kelly (<a href="#ref-kelly" class="citation-link">2020</a>) emphasizes enduring territorial control, drawing on the classical theories of Halford Mackinder and Alfred Thayer Mahan. He highlights intensifying competition over strategic <span class="term-chip" data-term="chokepoints">chokepoints</span>—such as the Strait of Hormuz, the Bab el-Mandeb, and the Malacca Strait—as well as competition over resource-rich regions and the strategic militarization of the South China Sea.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="case-card">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">Richard Baldwin &bull; The Second Unbundling &amp; ICT Fragmentation</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">Trade Economics</span>
      </div>
      <p>
        Baldwin (<a href="#ref-baldwin" class="citation-link">2016</a>) identifies Information and Communications Technology (ICT) as the core driver of the <span class="term-chip" data-term="second_unbundling">"Second Unbundling"</span>—the geographic separation of manufacturing processes across international borders. While dramatically reducing coordination costs and raising efficiency, this deep fragmentation introduces severe vulnerabilities, including intellectual property theft, cyber-espionage, and data localization mandates, ultimately contributing to <span class="term-chip" data-term="slowbalization">slowbalization</span>.
      </p>
    </div>

    <div class="case-card">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">Dani Rodrik &bull; The Globalization Trilemma</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">Political Economy</span>
      </div>
      <p>
        Rodrik (<a href="#ref-rodrik-2007" class="citation-link">2007</a>, <a href="#ref-rodrik-2011" class="citation-link">2011</a>) formulated the inescapable <span class="term-chip" data-term="trilemma">Globalization Trilemma</span>: nations cannot simultaneously achieve deep economic integration (hyperglobalization), democratic governance, and national sovereignty. They must choose two. This structural impossibility explains the widespread domestic political backlash in democratic nations and the rising imperative of <span class="term-chip" data-term="defensive_globalization">defensive globalization</span>.
      </p>
    </div>

  <section id="chap-history" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section III</span>
      <h2>Historical Turns: From Post-War Reconstruction to Ideological Weaponry</h2>
    </div>

    <!-- Primer Box -->
    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">A brief history of trade: optimism, inequality, and weapons of policy</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        After World War II, global organizations like the World Bank and IMF were created to rebuild ruined countries. While the Western world got rich, developing nations were largely left out. During the Cold War, trade wasn't just about selling goods—it was a weapon used to reward political allies and choke off enemies. When the Soviet Union fell in 1991, everyone expected globalization to solve world poverty. But opening markets too fast left many vulnerable societies damaged instead of prosperous.
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      The history of global trade and investment is full of turns, shaped by politics as much as by economic ideas. After World War II, institutions like the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the International Bank for Reconstruction and Development (World Bank) were meant to rebuild shattered economies. It was a moment full of optimism (<a href="#ref-frieden" class="citation-link">Frieden, 2006</a>).
    </p>

    <p>
      However, while this period looked like the beginning of a golden age for international capitalism, it came with deep structural inequalities. Countries in the industrialized West were the biggest winners, while many developing nations were left behind (<a href="#ref-fishlow" class="citation-link">Fishlow, 1980</a>; <a href="#ref-raffer" class="citation-link">Raffer, 1987</a>).
    </p>

    <!-- Historical Turns Comparative Table -->

          </tr>
        </tbody>
      </table>
    </div>

    <p>
      Things became even more complicated when the Cold War began. Trade became a weapon of ideology. Preeg (<a href="#ref-preeg" class="citation-link">1974</a>) explains how trade policies were used strategically, building alliances and isolating rivals. Then came the collapse of the Soviet Union in 1991. Globalization offered a new promise: economic growth through openness, integration, and technology transfer.
    </p>

    <p>
      Yet, the outcomes were highly uneven. Developing economies that engaged indiscriminately without defensive domestic safety nets suffered de-industrialization and volatile financial contagion (<a href="#ref-stiglitz-2002" class="citation-link">Stiglitz, 2002</a>). The post-Cold War phase showed conclusively that globalization was not a one-size-fits-all solution.
    </p>

  </section>

  <section id="chap-crises" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section IV</span>
      <h2>Recent Crises: The Fragility of Interconnected Systems</h2>
    </div>

    <!-- Primer Box -->
    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">Three modern shocks that proved global systems are fragile</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        For years, business leaders believed that an interconnected world made conflicts impossible because countries were too commercially dependent on each other to fight. That theory has completely collapsed under three major shocks: the U.S.-China Trade War (tariffs between the two biggest economies), Russia's invasion of Ukraine (which weaponized natural gas and grain), and Brexit (the UK cutting itself off from the EU single market).
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Insight:</strong> In a tightly wired global system, an economic or military shock in one country instantly travels down the wire to spark inflation and food shortages across the entire planet.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      The early 2000s have made it clear that global trade and national control don't always move in the same direction. Some of the most critical events in recent history have shown how fragile and surprisingly resilient our interconnected world can be (<a href="#ref-krugman-1998" class="citation-link">Krugman, 1998</a>).
    </p>

      <!-- Tab 1: Trade War -->
      <div id="tab-trade-war" class="case-tab-content active">
        <div class="case-card">
          <div class="case-card-header">
            <span class="case-card-title">Bilateral Protectionism &amp; Tariff Escalation (2018–Present)</span>
            <span class="case-card-tag">Geoeconomic Decoupling</span>
          </div>
          <p>
            The U.S.-China trade war kicked off in 2018 when the two largest economies began hitting each other with tariffs. Initiated under Section 301 investigations into intellectual property practices and industrial subsidies, the conflict rapidly expanded from raw materials (steel and aluminum) to over $350 billion in manufactured consumer goods.
          </p>
          <p>
            The World Trade Organization (<a href="#ref-wto" class="citation-link">2022</a>) estimated that between 2018 and 2021, global GDP dropped by about <strong>0.5%</strong> because of this economic showdown. Rather than balancing bilateral deficits, the tariffs disrupted supply networks, diverted trade to secondary intermediaries (such as Vietnam and Mexico), and signaled the end of unconstrained bilateral commercial integration.
          </p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <!-- Tab 2: Ukraine Conflict -->
      <div id="tab-ukraine" class="case-tab-content">
        <div class="case-card">
          <div class="case-card-header">
            <span class="case-card-title">Weaponized Energy Pipelines &amp; The Black Sea Food Crisis</span>
            <span class="case-card-tag">Commodity Shock</span>
          </div>
          <p>
            Russia's invasion of Ukraine turned natural gas infrastructure (Nord Stream) and agricultural transit corridors into active tools of strategic pressure. The fighting immediately halted operations across the Black Sea grain trade, severely restricting exports from two of the world's most vital agricultural suppliers.
          </p>
          <p>
            The UNCTAD (<a href="#ref-unctad" class="citation-link">2021</a>, 2022) reported a <strong>20% spike in global wheat prices</strong> immediately after the war started, triggering acute food insecurity across import-dependent nations in North Africa and the Middle East. The message was unmistakable: modern conflicts don't stay local; weaponized interdependence converts geographic chokepoints into worldwide humanitarian and price shocks.
          </p>
        </div>
      </div>

      <!-- Tab 3: Brexit -->
      <div id="tab-brexit" class="case-tab-content">
        <div class="case-card">
          <div class="case-card-header">
            <span class="case-card-title">Sovereignty vs. Market Access: The Cost of De-Integration</span>
            <span class="case-card-tag">Institutional Separation</span>
          </div>
          <p>
            When the United Kingdom voted to leave the European Union, it stepped away from the most advanced, frictionless regional economic partnership in the world. Driven by populist demands to "take back control" of national borders and laws, the UK opted out of the single market and customs union.
          </p>
          <p>
            The long-term economic costs are clear: the UK's decision to leave the single market <strong>reduced inward foreign investment by 11%</strong> and left its regulatory systems more fragmented. It provided a concrete empirical demonstration of Rodrik's trilemma: reclaiming total national sovereignty inevitably creates severe transaction costs and market friction for domestic businesses.
          </p>
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

  <section id="chap-development" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section V</span>
      <h2>The Development Gap &amp; Adaptive Sovereignty</h2>
    </div>

    <!-- Primer Box -->
    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">Who pays the price when big nations clash?</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        When superpowers enter trade wars or cut off pipelines, wealthy nations can cushion the blow with government subsidies and cash reserves. Developing countries cannot. As global investors pull money out of risky frontiers to invest in "near-shoring" (moving factories closer to home or among allies), poorer nations lose access to export markets and critical investments.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concept:</strong> <span class="term-chip" data-term="development_as_freedom">Adaptive Sovereignty</span>, rooted in Amartya Sen's philosophy, teaches that the best defense against world trade shocks isn't high tariff walls, but investing in schools, clinics, and strong institutions that allow citizens to adapt quickly.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      When global tensions rise, developing nations often pay the steepest price. They lose access to key export markets and face even more food and energy insecurity than before. At the same time, the multilateral rules that once helped keep international trade predictable and fair are starting to break down. The World Trade Organization has noted that more countries are turning away from universal rules toward bilateral, regional deals and one-sided trade moves (<a href="#ref-wto" class="citation-link">WTO, 2022</a>).
    </p>

    <p>
      In recent years, foreign direct investment (FDI) has started to favor regions seen as politically and geographically stable—a strategic shift known as <span class="term-chip" data-term="near_shoring">"near-shoring"</span> and "friend-shoring." This imbalance creates widening global hierarchies of wealth. As Thomas Piketty (<a href="#ref-piketty" class="citation-link">2014</a>) shows, wealth keeps accumulating faster than the overall economy grows ($r > g$). The result is a widening structural gap between capital-rich asset owners and those who rely solely on wages.
    </p>

    <div class="case-card" style="margin: 1.75rem 0; border-left: 4px solid #047857;">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">Adaptive Sovereignty: The Human Development Alternative</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">Amartya Sen Framework</span>
      </div>
      <p>
        The challenges that come with rising geopolitical tensions don't have easy solutions. Tackling them requires smart, flexible policies that work both at home and through international cooperation. At the national level, strong democratic institutions are one of the best defenses.
      </p>
      <p>
        Amartya Sen’s (<a href="#ref-sen" class="citation-link">1999</a>) view of <span class="term-chip" data-term="development_as_freedom">"development as freedom"</span> argues that policies promoting public education, healthcare, and civil liberties make societies far more adaptable and resilient to external economic turbulence.
      </p>
      <div class="case-card-quote" style="border-left-color: #047857;">
        <strong>Strategic Takeaway:</strong> Integrating into the world economy successfully sometimes means not jumping in all at once. Strategic, gradual engagement can actually help countries grow stronger and build institutional capacity before taking on the full pressures of global financial markets.
      </div>
    </div>

  </section>

  <section id="chap-future" class="monograph-chapter">
    <div class="chapter-title-header">
      <span class="chapter-number-tag">Section VI</span>
      <h2>Technology, Climate, &amp; The New World Order</h2>
    </div>

    <!-- Primer Box -->
    <div class="primer-card">
      <div class="primer-card-header">
        <span class="primer-card-badge">💡 The Big Picture</span>
      </div>
      <div class="primer-card-title">The twin forces remaking the next fifty years</div>
      <div class="primer-card-body">
        The future of globalization will be decided by two unstoppable forces: advanced digital technology and climate change. Tools like artificial intelligence, 3D printing, and automated microchip plants could bring manufacturing back home to local cities. Meanwhile, extreme weather, water shortages, and mass migration will force governments to replace old trade treaties with green investment pacts that balance profit against planetary limits.
        <div class="primer-analogy">
          <strong>Key Concept:</strong> <span class="term-chip" data-term="green_pacts">Green Investment Pacts</span> (proposed by Joseph Stiglitz) connect trade privileges directly to clean energy standards and environmental preservation.
        </div>
      </div>
    </div>

    <p>
      The next few decades are set to reshape the global economy in big ways. Economist Richard Baldwin (<a href="#ref-baldwin" class="citation-link">2016</a>) suggests that thanks to digital tools like Artificial Intelligence and 3D printing, we might see more localized, automated manufacturing. This shift can dramatically reduce dependence on long, vulnerable global supply chains, but it also threatens to create new forms of technology-based inequality between nations possessing advanced algorithmic intellectual property and those without.
    </p>

    <!-- Editorial Figure 2: Semiconductor Manufacturing -->
    <figure class="monograph-figure">
      <img src="{{ '/assets/images/semiconductor-chip.jpg' | relative_url }}" alt="High-tech robotic arm manufacturing silicon wafers in semiconductor cleanroom">
      <figcaption class="monograph-caption">
        <span><strong>Figure 6.1:</strong> Advanced Semiconductor Fabrication &amp; Automated Microchip Manufacturing.</span>
        <span>Source: Advanced Industrial Microelectronics Photobank</span>
      </figcaption>
    </figure>

    <p>
      Simultaneously, climate change is actively redrawing the global geopolitical map. Environmental pressures such as acute water shortages, agricultural desertification, and climate-induced migration are turning borders and transit corridors into volatile conflict zones.
    </p>

    <p>
      Joseph Stiglitz (<a href="#ref-stiglitz-2007" class="citation-link">2007</a>) suggests that navigating the transition to a low-carbon economy will require a new kind of global architecture—replacing outdated, tariff-focused trade agreements with binding <span class="term-chip" data-term="green_pacts">green investment pacts</span>. This path demands difficult structural choices: balancing efficiency with resilience, and economic growth with ecological and environmental limits.
    </p>

    <!-- Conclusion Synthesis Callout Box -->
    <div class="case-card" style="margin-top: 2rem; border-left: 4px solid var(--accent-color); background: var(--bg-card);">
      <div class="case-card-header">
        <span class="case-card-title">Conclusion: Reconstructing Interdependence</span>
        <span class="case-card-tag">The Emerging Order</span>
      </div>
      <p>
        The globalization that emerges from this period of upheaval will differ fundamentally from its late-20th-century predecessor. It will likely be:
      </p>
      <ul style="margin: 0.75rem 0 1rem 1.5rem; font-size: 0.95rem; line-height: 1.7;">
        <li><strong>More regionalized</strong> in its physical supply chains and manufacturing clusters.</li>
        <li><strong>More conditional</strong> in its market access, requiring compliance with national security and carbon standards.</li>
        <li><strong>More explicit</strong> about the political values, institutional norms, and security alignments embedded in economic relationships.</li>
      </ul>
      <p>
        The task ahead is not to abandon economic interdependence, but to reconstruct it on realistic foundations that acknowledge the realities of geopolitical competition while preserving space for international cooperation where common interests align.
      </p>
    </div>
